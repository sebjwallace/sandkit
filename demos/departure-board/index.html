Goal: design what a station's departure board shows for each train, by changing the rules yourself and watching every train on the board follow.

Programming the board

The board knows a few facts about each train: whether it's running, how late it is, whether its platform is confirmed and how soon it's due. Rules turn those facts into what the board shows, tried from the top for each part of the board, and the first that matches applies. Program them as tables or as a graph: the two are the same rules, drawn two ways.

Try: make the board say Boarding, flashing, for trains due now: press + Rule, click its Due cell until it says now, and choose Boarding with the tone flash. Switch the trains to rush hour to see your rule against a different day. Then open Rules as a graph and pin Luton in the Highlights panel to see its path through.

Here you program a station's departure board yourself. Trains come in as data, rules decide what the board shows, and changing them needs no code and no agent.
The trains, as the board receives them. Change any of them, or add your own, and the board follows. On the right are the four facts the board works out from each one: running or not, how late, whether its platform is settled, how soon it's due. The rules only ever see these facts.
Each set of trains is a moment at the station. Switch between them to see the same rules on a quiet afternoon, in a rush hour, or for the last trains. Your changes to each are kept apart from the original.
The board, as passengers see it. Every line on it is decided by the rules just below.
Oxford is 12 minutes late. Its rules are lit: E3 shows Exp 14:20 and N3 the message. Point at any train to see the rules that decided it.
There's a tab of rules for each part of the board. On the left of the line are the conditions, on the right what the board shows, drawn as the board draws it. A table is read top to bottom, and the first rule whose conditions all match applies. A dash matches anything, so the last rule catches whatever's left.
Each rule counts the trains it decides. A rule that decides none says never, which usually means a rule above it catches those trains first.
