# Departure board: notes

A first try at a user programmable interface (the idea and the path we took are in `README.md`): people change a domain's logic themselves, without the agent. The domain is a station's departure board: trains come in as data, the board shows times, platforms, delays and messages, and the logic between them is what people program.

Open `index.html` by double-clicking it. Under the board, three tabs: the same rules programmed two ways, and the trains.

- **Rules as tables** (`tables.js`): a decision table per part of the board, in tabs of their own.
- **Rules as a graph** (`graph.js`): a flow from left to right, a train's details, the facts worked out from them, the rules in the order they're tried, and what the board shows, joined by wires. Conditions are wires dragged from a fact's value onto a rule; highlighting a train lights its path through.
- **Trains**: the input data, edited in place.

The tables and the graph are two interfaces onto one mechanism: both edit the same rules through `change()` and run them through `runBoard`. A second page, **By example** (`example.html`), programs the same rules without touching them: click a train, pick a part of the board, choose what it should show. The model (`widest` and `withRule` in `model.js`) proposes a rule with the train's facts as conditions, drops each condition it can while every example still holds, and places it just above the rule that decides that train now. Before adding it you see which other trains would change and whether any earlier example breaks, and can switch conditions on or off. Examples are kept, with the facts they were given for, and checked against the rules from then on, so a change on the rules page that breaks one shows here.

`board.js` holds what they share: saved state, the board, the trains, changing rules and highlighting.

## How it's built

- **`model.js` is the principled model:** the facts the board can know about a train (state, delay, platform, due), each with a few values, and what each part of the board can show. A configuration is plain data: four decision tables, read top to bottom, first match wins. `ORIGINAL` is the board as found, never changed.
- **The trains are the scenarios, in input presets.** Three moments at the station (`DATA`): a quiet afternoon chosen to cover the cases, a rush hour with a signal failure, and the last trains. Every change re-runs the chosen set, so the same rules can be tried against a different day. The trains are edited in the **Trains** tab: change any field, add or remove trains. Edits go into a working copy of each set, kept in the browser; the set itself is never changed, and the board is sorted by time as a real one is.
- **The page is plain HTML,** with the trains and the rules in `sb-tabs` just under the board, so a change and its effect are on screen together. The working configuration and chosen trains are kept in the viewer's browser.

## What it reveals

- Trains showing something different from the original are marked on the board, with what they showed before.
- Hidden trains stay on the board, faint and struck through, so hiding is never invisible.
- Each rule counts the trains it decides; a rule that decides none says **never**, which shows a rule shadowed by one above it.
- Pointing at a train lights the rules that decided it and its data; pointing at a rule lights the trains it decides.
- Changed cells and new rules are outlined against the original.
- **Pinned highlights.** A floating Highlights panel (`sb-panel`) lists every ref on the page, trains and rules. Pointing at one lights it on the page, and pointing at the page lights it in the panel. Each has two pins, a fill and an outline, set apart so a ref can be kept filled, outlined, both or neither (one click toggling a fill lost the outline people expected to keep). Pinned refs stay lit through every click and edit, and are worked out afresh after each change, so a pinned train's lit rules follow whichever rules now decide it.

## The tour

`sb-tour` explains the page on a first visit, or from **Take the tour**. Steps light the train or rule they explain (`data-light`), and two wait for the viewer to program something (`data-check`): adding a Boarding rule, then moving it up until Luton boards. Rendering redraws the tables, so after every change the page points the tour at its step again and rechecks it.

## Findings so far

- **Outputs drawn as the board draws them.** In the tables, what's shown and its tone use the board's own look (amber on black, tones in their colours, flash flashing), so there's no mapping from a table cell to the board. Conditions are pills, like the facts on the trains.
- **A divide between When and Then.** A gap with a line through it, and the two group headings, separate conditions from outputs. Hacked in here; a candidate for a decision table component.
- **The rules right under the board** make changes feel live. Floating panels were tried first; tabs under the board were simpler and kept everything in one place.
- **Inputs beside the rules, not in a modal.** A modal was suggested for the trains, but it would hide the board while editing; a tab keeps the effect in view.
- **First match makes order matter, and people will find that out by playing.** A "Boarding" rule added above the fallback catches Brighton but not Luton, which is late and due now, because the delay rule sits above it.
- **Pinning is a click that lasts,** so it keeps to "pointing shows, clicking acts". It matters once a page is for doing, not only looking: hover highlights vanish the moment you move to change something. A candidate for Sandkit: a highlights panel over `sb-highlight` refs.
- **Name and home, if it becomes a component:** "Highlights" rather than "legend" (a legend explains symbols; this is the things on the page). Its own component, put in the shell's header through a slot like the modes, and included by the template, so every sandbox has it without the shell knowing it by name.
- **The graph shows why, the tables show what.** Following one train through the wires answers "why does Luton say this?" at a glance, which the tables only answer by pointing. The tables are denser and easier to scan for the whole set of rules.
- **A highlight is a trace in the graph.** Pointing at or pinning a train lights its whole path, details to facts to the conditions met to the rule applied to what it shows: the scenario, end to end. A rule lights its conditions, its wire and its output. Pins of several trains overlap, which compares scenarios at a glance. The graph first also followed one chosen train (Brighton by default) in green; alongside pins that was noise, so it went: the graph shows only what's highlighted. The page names its own parts (`data-part`) and says what each ref traces (`tracePartsOf`), so the shared highlighting stays generic.
- **Parts are refs too, written as paths, and the Highlights panel becomes an outliner.** A train opens into its inputs (`T3.delay`) and what the board shows for it (`T3.board.expected`); a rule into its conditions (`E3.when.delay`) and result (`E3.then`). Each can be pointed at and pinned. An input traces downstream (the facts it feeds, the rules that used them, what they showed); a board output traces upstream (the rule that decided it, its conditions, the inputs behind them, including inputs that fill its text, such as the reason). Reading the scenario both ways is the gain: "what does this field affect?" and "why does it say this?". The trace is worked out once in `board.js` (`traceOf`), and each page lights it with its own marks (`data-ref` for parts, `partsOf` for the graph's wires and sockets).
- **Pins can glow.** A Glow switch in the Highlights panel makes everything pinned fade gently in and out (a slow ease, not a blink), so pinned parts draw the eye while you work elsewhere. Every highlight colour goes through two variables built from one animated number (`--glow`), so one animation covers board, tables, graph and outliner alike. Off for people who ask for reduced motion.
- **An example is a fragment.** Each part of the board is decided on its own, so an example is one part for one train ("Brighton's Expected column says Boarding"); a scenario is several fragments of the same train. This made the page simple: one rule per example.
- **One example alone over-reaches.** The widest rule consistent with a single example has no conditions at all, and every on-time train boards. Treating the other trains on the board as they are now as examples too ("Leave the other trains as they are", on by default) gives a sensible first guess, Brighton's becoming "running and due now", and people widen it from there, seeing who changes. Switching it off shows how far one example would reach.
- **Examples are the specification; the rules are derived.** Kept examples work as tests: resetting the rules marks them broken. That's a different, maybe better, thing to agree with stakeholders than rules: what the board should say for trains they recognise.
- **The scenario catalog** (`catalog.html`): all 108 combinations of the four facts, each a card with a code (`SC-001`…, numbered by the combination, so codes survive rule changes), its line on the board, the train's details, and Given / When / Then in `sb-scenario`. Cards are grouped into families by the rules they pass through (17 with the original rules). A scenario is seen (shown with the real train that has those facts), given (an example, checked) or only inferred (shown with a made-up train built to have exactly those facts). What a family's scenarios share is what defines it ("Given state: cancelled"). First look: 97 of 108 scenarios are only inferred, and the cancelled family holds 36 scenarios whose other facts never matter: a family is the compressed form of its scenarios, and the catalog could show it that way rather than listing every member. "Family" isn't a formal term; the testing word is equivalence class.
- **The catalog as a notebook, with search, facets and sort.** Each family is an `sb-notebook` section (★ Key when anything in it was seen or given, collapsed when it's all inferred), so the notebook's outline, ▲ ▼ and expand/collapse come free, and it counts each family's `SC-` codes. Above it: free-text search over codes, trains, facts, what the board shows and rules; facets for where a scenario comes from and each fact, with counts that follow the other choices (within a facet choices widen, across facets they narrow); and a sort for the families. The notebook's filters are about sections, so scenario-level filtering sits outside it and rebuilds its sections; a notebook option to filter inside sections may be worth having.
- **Choosing a line, as in selective breeding** (the By example page's second tab, after Picbreeder). For one train, its line as the board shows it now among 8 variations; clicking one makes it the parent of the next generation; Back and New variations; Use this line turns each part that differs into an example, with the widest rule that leaves the other trains as they are. People choose rather than say: the simplest way in yet. Purely random variations were mostly nonsense ("On time" beside "Cancelled due to…"), and once a hidden line was picked nearly every child stayed hidden, so half of all variations borrow a part from a neighbouring scenario (what the rules say when one fact is different), and hiding is rare. Variations drawn from the principled space, rather than from chance, are what make it work. Choosing needs the input beside it: a Given / When / Then card (`sb-scenario`) with the train's details sits beside the population, its Then following the chosen line and saying what each changed part shows now.
- **A map of the space of lines, with the path through it.** Under the population, a 2D slice of the lines for one train: the Expected column across and the message down, each ordered by meaning with its four tones side by side (28 × 28 = 784 lines), the platform and showing held as chosen. Pointing previews that line in a board snippet, so moving the pointer changes it step by step; clicking chooses it. Overlaid: where it is now, the current population, and the path of choices, one segment per generation; points whose platform or showing differ from the slice are faded. It's a design-space grid, not a semantic space: the axes are ordered by meaning, but distance isn't measured meaning, and the other parts are held still. A semantic layout needs a measure of how differently people read two lines.
