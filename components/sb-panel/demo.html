Let viewers arrange a sandbox their own way, and put it back with one click.

About

A sandbox's layout is a good starting point, but exploring is personal: one person wants the rules beside the model, another wants the log large and the explanation out of the way. sb-panel lets viewers rearrange a sandbox as they work. Pop a panel out and it floats as a window that can be dragged, resized, collapsed or made full screen; dock it and it returns to the place the sandbox gave it. While it floats, a placeholder holds its place, so nothing else on the page moves.

Where it helps

  • Keeping something in view. Float the rules, the log or a chart beside whatever part of the sandbox you're exploring.
  • Focusing. Make one panel full screen to study it, or collapse the ones you're not using.
  • Comparing. Pull two panels side by side, even when the sandbox put them far apart.

Where something else fits better

  • Content that only makes sense in place, such as a paragraph that introduces the figure below it. Leave it unwrapped.
  • Phones. Floating windows need room. This is designed for desktop and tablet screens.

Getting started

<script src="components/sb-panel/sb-panel.js"></script>

<sb-panel id="notes" heading="Notes">
  …anything: text, a table, a canvas, another component…
</sb-panel>

With an id, each viewer's arrangement is remembered when they come back.

Making it reveal, not just rearrange

  • Wrap the parts people will want beside each other, such as the rules and the model, or the log and the diagram.
  • Choose clear headings. A floating panel is known only by its title.
  • Lay the page out well first. Docked is how most people will see it; floating is for when they need something different.

Reading with a glossary

A long explanation with a glossary beside it. Pop the glossary out and drag it next to the paragraph you're reading.

A flock of starlings turning as one looks choreographed, yet no bird is in charge. Each follows a few local rules, reacting only to its nearest neighbours. Order at the scale of the flock emerges from interactions at the scale of the bird.

This is emergence: behaviour of the whole that no part intends. Ant trails, traffic jams and market prices arise the same way. None is designed; each is the sum of many small decisions made with local information.

Models of emergence are often agent-based: they simulate each individual and let the pattern appear. Their power is that the rules are simple enough to state in a sentence, and the results rich enough to surprise the person who wrote them.

That surprise is the point. A model that only confirms what its author expected teaches little. One that produces something nobody anticipated invites the best question a sandbox can raise: why did that happen?
