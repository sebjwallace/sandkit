A decision as a card: the options weighed, the one chosen, the reason, who decided and when.

About

A decision is the moment a group settles something: what they weighed, what they chose, why, and who was there. This card shows one, with the options turned down kept in view, because what was rejected and why is half of a decision's value. When people's names are on the choices that shaped a model, they can see why it is the way it is, and own it.

Where it helps

  • Workshops with the people who know the domain. Record a decision as the group makes it, with their names on it, and save it as source material.
  • Explaining why a model is the way it is. A decision about a rule or a setting, cited by it, answers "why this?" months later.
  • Seeing how thinking changed. A decision that replaces an earlier one says so, and the earlier one stays, faded, with what replaced it.

Where something else fits better

  • A rule the model follows, and whether it holds: a rule card. A decision is how a rule came to be.
  • A question still open, or a short note: a callout.
  • Choices that branch on answers down to an outcome: a decision tree.

Getting started

Give the card a decision. Only id, title and status are needed, so a proposal can start with just its options. See the manifest for the full contract.

card.decision = {
  id: 'DEC-3',
  title: 'Which imagery should map the flood?',
  status: 'accepted',
  when: '2026-10-20T07:45',
  deciders: ['Mateo Ruiz', 'Hana Kobayashi'],
  options: [
    { id: 'a', title: 'Optical', pros: ['Sharp'], cons: ['Blocked by cloud'] },
    { id: 'b', title: 'Radar', pros: ['Sees through cloud'], cons: ['Coarser'] }
  ],
  chosen: 'b',
  reason: 'We map the flood with radar. Cloud stays all day.'
};

Set editable to let a group record decisions. The card never keeps what's typed: Propose and Accept fire sb-save with the whole decision, and the sandbox saves it and sets it back.

Making it reveal, not just record

  • Ask, then answer. Write the title as the question the group faced and open the reason with the answer, so the card can be read in two lines.
  • Keep the options turned down. Their cons are often the real reason for the choice.
  • Name the people. A decision with names and a time is evidence; the card says so when they're missing.
  • Tie it to the model. List what it's about, and have those items cite it, so pointing at one finds the other.
  • Replace, don't edit. Reopen an accepted decision to start a new one, so the story of how it changed is kept.
