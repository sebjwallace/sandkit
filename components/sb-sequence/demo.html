Who says what to whom, in what order, played a step at a time.

About

Much of how the world works is a conversation: you ask, someone answers, they ask someone else, and eventually something comes back. A sequence diagram lays that conversation out. Everyone involved stands along the top, time runs down the page, and each message is an arrow from whoever sends it to whoever receives it. Playing it a message at a time shows how the work passes from hand to hand.

Where it helps

  • Following who does what, in order. Ordering food, booking an appointment, a payment going through, two computers talking.
  • Seeing the waiting. A reply only comes after everything it depends on, which shows why some things take as long as they do.
  • Telling a process as a story. Step through it with the sandbox, adding a note where something needs explaining.

Where something else fits better

  • Branches and loops. A sequence diagram shows one way through. When the choices are the point, use a flowchart.
  • A long record of what happened. For hundreds of messages, a log reads better.
  • How things are connected, rather than what they say to each other: use a graph.

Getting started

<script src="components/sb-sequence/sb-sequence.js"></script>
<sb-sequence numbered></sb-sequence>

<script>
  const seq = document.querySelector('sb-sequence');
  seq.participants = [{ id: 'you', label: 'You' }, { id: 'cafe', label: 'Café' }];
  seq.messages = [
    { id: 'ask', from: 'you', to: 'cafe', label: 'A flat white, please' },
    { kind: 'note', over: 'cafe', label: 'Makes the coffee' },
    { id: 'give', from: 'cafe', to: 'you', label: 'Here you go', kind: 'reply' }
  ];
  seq.current = 'ask';
</script>

Messages are drawn in the order given. A call (the default) is a solid line with a filled arrow; a reply is dashed; an async message, sent without waiting, has an open arrow. A message to oneself loops back. A note sits over one participant, or across several.

Making it reveal, not just display

  • Play it. Set current to the message happening now; the ones still to come fade, so the story unfolds.
  • Add notes for the why, such as a rule being applied or time passing, not for what the arrows already say.
  • Use tone for what goes wrong, such as a refusal or an error, and only that.
  • Point at it from the prose. Set highlight when a sentence mentions a participant or a message.
