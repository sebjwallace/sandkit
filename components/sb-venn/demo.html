Two or three sets as overlapping circles, with every region they make counted, named and ready to point at.

About

Whenever things fall into groups that overlap, the interesting part is usually the overlap: patients with both conditions, animals that break the rules, the skills only one person on a team has. A Venn diagram draws the groups as circles, so every combination gets a place of its own. Lists can tell you who is in each group; only the diagram shows at a glance which combinations are crowded, which are rare, and which are empty.

Where it helps

  • Classification and its exceptions. Sort things by two or three properties and the odd ones out sit where nobody expected them, or where the rule says nothing should be.
  • Probability and screening. Give counts instead of things, and the diagram shows how a small overlap compares with everything around it.
  • Comparing lists. What two suppliers both stock, which features three products share, what's in this version but not the last.
  • Explaining logic. "And", "or" and "not" are regions: point at the words and the regions light up.

Where something else fits better

  • Four or more sets. The regions get too small to read. Put the things in a table with a column per set and let viewers filter.
  • Groups inside groups. When every set sits wholly inside another, it's a hierarchy: a tree shows it better.
  • Exact sizes. The circles are all the same size, whatever they hold. When the proportions are the point, compare the counts as bars.

Getting started

<script src="components/sb-venn/sb-venn.js"></script>
<sb-venn universe="All animals"></sb-venn>

<script>
  const venn = document.querySelector('sb-venn');
  venn.sets = [{ id: 'm', label: 'Mammals' }, { id: 'w', label: 'Live in water' }];
  venn.items = model.animals;                       // [{ id, label, sets: ['m', 'w'] }]
  // or, with no things to name:  venn.counts = { m: 40, 'm&w': 6, w: 120, '': 900 };

  venn.highlight = { regions: ['m&w'] };            // the overlap
</script>

Each region has a ref naming exactly the sets it's in, so 'm&w' is the overlap, 'm' is mammals that don't live in water, and '' is everything outside both. Set items or counts again whenever the model changes, and whatever changed region is marked. Pointing fires sb-point and clicking sb-select, each with the set, region or item.

Making it reveal, not just display

  • Point at regions from the prose. "Mammals that lay eggs" is a region with a ref: let the words light it up, and the line under the diagram reads it out in full.
  • Keep the empty regions. A region marked "none" is often the finding: nothing is both. Choose sets whose emptiness would mean something.
  • Change the rule, not the picture. When a definition shifts, set the items again: the ones that changed region are marked, and pointing at one says where it was.
  • Count the whole population. With a universe and its outside region counted, small overlaps are seen against everything, which is where intuition most often fails.

Examples

Animals that break the rules

Most mammals give birth to live young and live on land, which is why mammals that lay eggs are such a surprise: there are only two kinds, and the platypus also lives in water. Is anything in all three? Point at a region to read it out, and click an animal to learn why it's there. Then change what counts as living in water.

What a positive test means

A thousand people are screened for a condition. The test catches most people who have it, and wrongly flags only a few who don't. So if your test is positive, you very likely have it? Compare people who are ill and tested positive with people who tested positive but aren't ill. When the condition is rare, the healthy are so many that their few false alarms outnumber the true ones.
