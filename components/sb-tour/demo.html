A guided tour of the page as it stands, one part at a time.

About

Someone opening a sandbox for the first time doesn't know where to look. A tour shows them: a tooltip beside one part, saying what it is and why it matters, with the rest of the page dimmed, and Next moving on to the following part, scrolling to it and opening whatever hides it. Because each step only names what it points at, a tour is a few lines to write, and it keeps working as the page changes.

Where it helps

  • How to use the sandbox. "These run the model", "this table marks the rule that just fired".
  • A chapter's key parts, pointed at in turn.
  • A demonstration. A step can wait until the viewer does something, such as pressing Play, before moving on to what happened.

Where something else fits better

  • A story told across several views, laid out side by side: use a walkthrough, which owns its layout. A tour owns nothing on the page; it points at the page as it already is.
  • Explaining one thing. A note beside it, or a code's card, says it without taking over the screen.
  • Finding where a thing appears. A cross-reference lists every place; a tour goes to the places its author chose.

Getting started

<script src="components/sb-tour/sb-tour.js"></script>

<sb-tour id="basics" heading="How this works">
  <div data-step>Here's how to explore it.</div>
  <div data-step data-at="controls">These run the model.</div>
  <div data-step data-at="RUL-014" data-wait>Press Play, and watch this rule fire.</div>
</sb-tour>

<script>
  tour.addEventListener('sb-step', (e) => {
    tour.anchor = e.detail.ref ? goTo(e.detail.ref) || null : null;
  });
  startButton.addEventListener('click', () => tour.start());
</script>

The tour never searches the page. On each step, the sandbox goes to the step's ref with goTo from the locating practice, which opens closed tabs and folded branches on the way, and hands the tour the element it found. A step waiting on the viewer moves on once the sandbox sets ready.

Making it reveal, not just point

  • Say why, not only what. "This marks the rule that just fired, so you can see why a store ordered" teaches more than "This is the rules table".
  • Let people act. A waiting step that asks them to press something, then shows what changed, is worth several that only describe.
  • Keep it short. Five or so steps; a long tour is a manual.
  • Fix what it can't find. A step whose part has gone says so, like an unresolved code: a reference to put right.
