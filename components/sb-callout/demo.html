Definitions, tips, questions and warnings that stand out from the text.

About

Good textbooks set certain things apart: a definition in a box, a warning in the margin, a question to stop and think about. Readers find them at a glance, and come back to them later. sb-callout does the same for a sandbox. Each kind has its own colour and icon, so readers soon learn what to expect, and answers can stay hidden until a reader has thought for themselves.

Where it helps

  • Definitions of the terms a chapter introduces, easy to find again.
  • Questions with hidden answers, so readers try before they look.
  • Warnings about a common misunderstanding, or a limit of the model.

Where something else fits better

  • Ordinary explanation. Most of a chapter should be plain text; callouts only stand out against it.
  • Long content. A callout is a box for a few lines. A long explanation belongs in the page, or its own chapter.

Making it reveal, not just decorate

  • Put the definition where the idea first appears, right after the reader has seen it in action.
  • Ask questions the sandbox can answer. "Drag w onto v's line: what happens to the span?" sends people back to explore.
  • Hide answers, so the moment of working it out isn't taken away.

Examples

A page from a textbook

A definition between paragraphs, a tip inside a list, and a warning.

Water moves continuously between the oceans, the air and the land. The sun drives it: warming the sea, lifting vapour into the sky, where it cools and falls again.

The change of liquid water into vapour, as the sun's heat gives water molecules enough energy to escape into the air.

Three things make evaporation faster:

  • Higher temperature.
  • Wind, carrying the moist air away.

    That's why washing dries faster on a breezy day than a still one.
  • Lower humidity.

Clouds aren't vapour. Vapour is invisible; clouds are tiny droplets of liquid water that have already condensed.

A quick quiz

Questions first; answers stay hidden until you open them.

Which falls faster in a vacuum: a hammer or a feather?

Neither. Without air to slow the feather, both fall at the same rate. Astronaut David Scott showed it on the Moon in 1971.

Why is the sky blue?

Air scatters short, blue wavelengths of sunlight far more than long, red ones, so blue light reaches us from every direction across the sky.
