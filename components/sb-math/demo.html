Equations whose parts light up the things they stand for.

About

An equation is a compact sentence about something else: a shape, a motion, a model. The hard part for a reader is keeping track of which symbol stands for which thing. sb-math lets the reader point: hover a term and every place that thing appears lights up, in the diagram, in the prose, in the numbers. Point at the diagram and the term lights up in return. The mathematics is ordinary MathML, which browsers draw natively, so there is nothing extra to load.

Where it helps

  • Formulas beside diagrams. Which side is c? Hover it and see.
  • Worked calculations, where each number in an equation comes from somewhere on the page.
  • Prose about an equation, where words like "the first column" should point at the symbols they mean.

Where something else fits better

  • Numbers people need to edit. An editable matrix or a set of sliders is better than an equation for changing values.
  • Long derivations. A page of algebra is still a page of algebra; point at the few terms that matter.

Getting started

<script src="components/sb-math/sb-math.js"></script>

<sb-math>
  <math><msup><mi data-ref="a">a</mi><mn>2</mn></msup><mo>+</mo><msup><mi data-ref="b">b</mi><mn>2</mn></msup></math>
</sb-math>

Anything else on the page can join in by listening for, or announcing, the page-wide sb-highlight event. The highlighting practice shows how.

Making it reveal, not just typeset

  • Mark the terms people ask about, not every symbol.
  • Make it work both ways. Pointing at the picture should light the equation too.
  • Match colours between terms and what they stand for, so lighting up confirms what colour already suggested.

Examples

Pythagoras

Hover a term in the equation, a side of the triangle, or a word in the sentence. All three point at the same things.

a2+ b2= c2

In a right-angled triangle, the squares on the two shorter sides, a and b, add up to the square on the longest side.

A circle's area

The radius appears once in the picture and twice in the formula. Point at either.

A=π·r·r

The area is π times the radius times itself.
