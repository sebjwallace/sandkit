A story told across several views, one step at a time.

About

Some things can't be explained by one picture. A card payment is a screen, a set of systems, some code and a string of messages, all at once; the moon's phases are where it is in space and what we see from here. A walkthrough puts the views side by side and tells the story across them, one step at a time, outlining the view to look at and lighting up the part being talked about.

Where it helps

  • Joining several views into one explanation, so readers see how a click on a screen becomes a line of code and a message to another system.
  • Guiding attention. Readers never wonder where to look: the step tells them, and they can point at its words to look again.
  • Opening a sandbox. A short walkthrough is a good first chapter, before readers explore on their own.

Where something else fits better

  • One view. Prose beside it, with words that point, is enough.
  • Free exploration. Once readers know their way, let them roam the views without steps.
  • Progress over time. A scrubber or play controls step a model; a walkthrough steps an explanation.

Getting started

<script src="components/sb-walkthrough/sb-walkthrough.js"></script>

<sb-walkthrough>
  <section data-step data-focus="screen" data-highlight="pay">
    <p>The shopper presses <span data-ref="pay">Pay</span>.</p>
  </section>
  <section data-step data-focus="code" data-highlight="charge">…</section>
  <div slot="screen">…</div>
  <div slot="code">…</div>
</sb-walkthrough>

<script>
  const walk = document.querySelector('sb-walkthrough');
  const show = (refs) => { /* set each view's highlight */ };
  show(walk.current.highlight);
  walk.addEventListener('sb-step', (e) => show(e.detail.highlight));
  walk.addEventListener('sb-point', (e) => show(e.detail.ref ? [e.detail.ref] : walk.current.highlight));
</script>

Children marked data-step are the narration; children with a slot are the views, one to four of them, laid out beside it (on the left, or along the bottom with narration="bottom"). Each step names the views to outline in data-focus and the refs to light in data-highlight. The walkthrough doesn't know what's in the views, so the sandbox passes the refs on, as above. Anything else a step should do, such as which line of code is running, goes on it as a data- attribute. Panels make good views: readers can pop one out and the outline follows it.

Making it reveal, not just display

  • One idea per step, and point at it. A step that lights nothing is usually two steps or none.
  • Move the reader's eye on purpose. Change the focused view when the story crosses from one view to another, and say so in the words.
  • Mark the words that name things, so readers can point back at them.
  • Leave the views working. Readers who stop to poke at a view learn the most.

The full contract, including every property, method, event and styling hook, is in the manifest.

Examples

Paying by card

Four views, one story. Step through it, and point at the underlined words to look again.

1 · The shopper pays

It starts on the checkout page. The shopper types their card number and presses Pay.

Everything after this happens in about two seconds, between four systems.

2 · The browser tells the shop

The browser sends the order to the shop's server. Not the card number itself: a token standing for it, which the payment provider gave the page.

3 · The shop asks for the money

The server never sees the card. It asks the provider to charge the token for the order's total, and waits.

Notice what happens if it's declined: nothing is saved, and the shopper is told.

4 · The bank decides

The provider asks the shopper's bank, which checks the balance and fraud rules, and approves.

5 · Back to the shopper

Approved, the server saves the order, and the page shows the confirmation. The money itself moves days later, between banks.

Why the moon has phases

The narration along the bottom, with two views beside each other. Each step carries the moon's place in its orbit as data-angle, and the sandbox moves both views to it.

Half the moon is always lit. The sun lights the half facing it, wherever the moon is. What changes is how much of that half we can see from Earth.

New moon. The moon sits between us and the sun, its lit half facing away. We see almost nothing.

First quarter. A quarter of the way round, we look at the moon side-on and see half its face lit, on the right.

Full moon. Opposite the sun, the whole lit half faces us. Compare where it is with what we see.

Last quarter. Three quarters round, the left half is lit, shrinking each night until the next new moon, about 29½ days after the last.
