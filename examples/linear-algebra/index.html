Goal: see linear algebra rather than memorise it: vectors as arrows, and matrices as ways of moving the whole of space.

Chapter 1 · Vectors

A vector is an arrow that starts at the origin. It is also a pair of numbers, and the two are the same thing: the numbers say how to reach the arrow's tip. (2, 1) means two steps right and one step up.

Adding two vectors means walking along one, then along the other. In numbers, add the first parts together and the second parts together.

Try: drag the tips of v and w. Watch where v + w lands, and check it against the numbers. Can you make the sum land back on the origin?

Drag w until it points exactly the opposite way to v, with the same length. Then w is −v, and walking v and then −v brings you straight back where you started.

Chapter 2 · Scaling, combinations and span

Multiplying a vector by a number stretches it. 2·v is twice as long; ½·v is half; −1·v points the opposite way. The number is called a scalar, because it scales.

Scale two vectors, then add them, and you have a linear combination: a·v + b·w. Every choice of a and b reaches some point. All the points you can reach this way are the span of v and w.

The span of some vectors is every point you can reach by scaling them and adding them up. Two vectors that point in different directions span the whole plane; two that line up span only a line.

Try: slide a and b until the tip lands on the red point P. Then show the span, and drag w until it lines up with v. What happens to everywhere you can reach?

Chapter 3 · Matrices as moving space

A matrix is a way of moving the whole of space, and it needs only two pairs of numbers to say how. Its first column is where , the arrow one step right, lands. Its second column is where , the arrow one step up, lands.

Why is that enough? Every vector is a combination of î and ĵ: (2, 1) is 2·î + 1·ĵ. Move î and ĵ, keep the same combination, and you know where every vector goes. The grid lines follow, staying straight, parallel and evenly spaced.

A way of moving space that keeps the origin in place and keeps grid lines straight, parallel and evenly spaced. Every one is described by a matrix, whose columns say where î and ĵ land.

Chapter 4 · Determinants

A matrix stretches some areas and squashes others, but it scales every area by the same amount. That amount is the determinant. Watch the unit square: it starts with area 1, so whatever area it ends up with is the determinant.

A negative determinant means space has been flipped over, like a page turned face down: ĵ ends up clockwise from î instead of anticlockwise. A determinant of zero means space has been squashed flat, onto a line or even a single point.

The factor by which a transformation scales every area. For the matrix with entries a, b in the top row and c, d in the bottom, it is a·d − b·c. Negative means space is flipped over; zero means it is squashed flat.

Try: drag where î and ĵ land. Can you make the square twice as big? Flip it over? Squash it flat? Point at the two halves of the formula to see which entries of the matrix each one uses.

Chapter 5 · One transformation, then another

Apply one matrix, then another, and space has moved in some new way. That new way is a transformation too, so it has a matrix of its own: the product of the two. Its columns are simply where î and ĵ end up after both moves.

The order matters. Turning and then shearing is not the same as shearing and then turning. That is why matrix multiplication doesn't let you swap the order the way ordinary multiplication does.

Try: play both moves. Then swap the order and play again: does space end up in the same place? Edit A and B to find two moves where the order doesn't matter.
